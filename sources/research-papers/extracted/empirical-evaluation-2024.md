The complexity of ICT infrastructures is continuously increasing, presenting a formidable challenge in
safeguarding them against cyber attacks. In light of escalating cyber threats and limited availability of expert
resources, organizations must explore more eﬃcient approaches to assess their resilience and undertake proactive
measures. Threat modeling is an eﬀective approach for assessing the cyber resilience of ICT systems. One method
is to utilize Attack Graphs, which visually represent the steps taken by adversaries during an attack. Previously,
MAL (the Meta Attack Language) was proposed, which serves as a framework for developing Domain-Speciﬁc
Languages (DSLs) and generating Attack Graphs for modeled infrastructures. coreLang is a MAL-based threat
modeling language that utilizes such Attack Graphs to enable attack simulations and security assessments for
the generic ICT domain. Developing domain-speciﬁc languages for threat modeling and attack simulations
provides a powerful approach for conducting security assessments of infrastructures. However, ensuring the
correctness of these modeling languages raises a separate research question. In this study we conduct an empirical
experiment aiming to falsify such a domain-speciﬁc threat modeling language. The potential inability to falsify
the language through our empirical testing would lead to its corroboration, strengthening our belief in its
validity within the parameters of our study. The outcomes of this approach indicated that, on average, the
assessments generated by attack simulations outperformed those of human experts. Additionally, both human
experts and simulations exhibited signiﬁcantly superior performance compared to random guessers in their
assessments. While speciﬁc human experts occasionally achieved better assessments for particular questions in
the experiments, the eﬃciency of simulation-generated assessments surpasses that of human domain experts.
### 1. Introduction
Protection of Information and Communications Technology (ICT)
infrastructures and ensuring their security is one of the most critical
challenges faced by our society today. Cybersecurity assessments play
a crucial role towards securing them. The idea is simple: it will be eas-
ier to protect once we understand the security gaps (vulnerabilities)
or shortcomings (weaknesses), the limits of our defenses, and what to
protect against. However, as systems are becoming large and complex,
this assessment is not a trivial task to undertake (Nobles, 2018). Formal
methods and automated analysis tools can help experts tackle this com-
plex problem. Indeed, for the task of assessing the security of large ICT
infrastructures, threat modeling has emerged as a promising approach,
and within this ﬁeld attack trees and attack graphs are key concepts.
In previous works the Meta Attack Language (Johnson et al., 2018)
(MAL) and coreLang (Katsikeas et al., 2020) were presented. MAL eases

### 2. Related work
In a paper by Yskout et al. (2020), one of the ﬁve challenges iden-
tiﬁed for improving the maturity of the threat modeling domain is the
need to deﬁne validated metrics for assessing the quality of a threat
modeling process and its outcomes. The authors argue that suitable met-
rics to accurately evaluate if a threat model reﬂects reality are currently
lacking.
Numerous recent empirical studies have explored the diﬃculty, ef-
fectiveness, and various facets of speciﬁc cyber-attacks, including ran-
somware attacks (Connolly et al., 2020) and zero-day attacks (Bilge and
Dumitraş, 2012), as well as a human-centered approach to threat mod-
eling that extends an existing framework and lays the groundwork for
additional empirical, human-centered studies (Ferro et al., 2021). How-
ever, there is limited research concerning the empirical validation of
threat modeling methods.
Considering the variety of threat modeling approaches available to-
day, an interesting question that can be raised is which approach would
be the most suitable for each modeling situation. For example, an at-
tempt to determine which one out of the attack graphs and fault tree
methods is more eﬀective in aiding cyber attack perception is empiri-
cally evaluated in Lallie et al. (2018). While Lallie et al. (2020) provides
an empirical survey of attack graphs and attack trees to identify how
attack graphs and attack trees present cyber attacks in terms of their
visual syntax.
A systematic literature review of threat modeling identiﬁed six dif-
ferent methods for validating threat models (Xiong and Lagerström,
2019). These methods include simulation, analysis, theoretical exam-
ples, empirical case studies, implementation, and expert review. The
validation approach in this paper falls under the categories of simula-
tion and expert review.
In a paper identiﬁed in the literature review by Xiong and Lager-
ström (2019), an expert review was conducted. The authors used a
threat model to identify threats that would indicate to an attacker the
presence of a honeypot (Dahbul et al., 2017). They enhanced the hon-
eypots to make them more diﬃcult to detect and asked two professional
penetration testers to identify the honeypots without any prior informa-
tion about the hosts. The authors compared their threat model against
the experts’ ﬁndings. This approach of comparing experts’ assessments
with the threat model is similar to the one presented in this paper. How-
ever, our study involves multiple experiments and gradually providing
information to the experts to further evaluate the threat model.
Similarly, in a study by Allodi et al. (2022), a novel and realistic
attacker model was presented and validated using a quantitative empir-
ical approach with a large dataset of attacks against over one million
real systems. However, a limited number of works focusing on the em-
pirical validation of threat modeling languages were found through a
literature search.
Furthermore, a systematic method for developing and evaluating
MAL-based domain-speciﬁc languages was proposed in Hacks et al.
(2022), where a survey revealed that only 9% of the developed MAL-
based DSLs had been evaluated by industry/security experts at that
time.
Regarding the validation of threat modeling languages speciﬁcally,
an example is the use of the Feigenbaum test (Katsikeas et al., 2022).
The Feigenbaum test (Feigenbaum, 2003) is a type of Turing test in
which a human judge determines if a behavior is caused by another
human or a computer, with the underlying objective being that the
computer aims to perform as well as a human, thereby demonstrat-
ing human-like intelligence and behavior. In this paper, the evaluation
involves both experts and a computer simulation, but we do not employ
a human judge to assess the results. Instead, we consider the penetra-
tion testing training data we have gathered as the reference value for
evaluation. To assess the simulation results and expert opinions against
the penetration testing training data, we utilize a method inspired by
the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) (Saaty, 1984). AHP allows for
the mathematical evaluation of the complex problem through pairwise
comparisons. In our study, we adopt a similar scale, as proposed on the
AHP article, but we instead focus on assessing the diﬃculty of achieving
diﬀerent hacking goals.
### 3. Background
#### 3.1. The cyber range
When evaluating the domain-speciﬁc threat modeling language, we
compare the assessment results to what we consider to be the ground
truth. That ground truth originates from the penetration testing training
data that was collected from a capture the ﬂag (CTF) exercise on a
virtual cyber range as part of an academic course at university level. In
order to produce comparable results, the exact same cyber range is used
by the human experts in order to evaluate the diﬃculty of the diﬀerent
attack scenarios, and for the attack simulation’s assessment a model of
that cyber range is created. The network topology of the deployed cyber
range is shown in Fig. 1.
The cyber range is deployed in Google Cloud and consists of 79 vir-
tual machine instances that are created and managed with Ansible. The
students have access to the cyber range via a VPN connection. The ex-
perts, on top of that, will additionally have access to the Google Cloud
management console. Through the console they will be able to directly
inspect the conﬁguration and get more information regarding the in-
frastructure.
In the cyber range, a number of previously detected cybersecurity
vulnerabilities and weaknesses are deployed. The ﬂags are text strings
that have been placed throughout the cyber range. These can only be
found when the exploitation of a vulnerability and/or weakness has
been successful. The ﬂags have been named with sequential numbers
but those numbers do not necessarily follow the order with which they
are found inside the cyber range. In Table 1, a detailed list, of all the
ﬂags and their corresponding topics can be found. One of the instances
is the VPN server and the 17 ﬂags are contained in six other instances.
The rest of the instances are honeypots and do not contain any ﬂags. The
instances are separated into two networks. One network is the external
(i.e. it is accessible from the VPN connection), and behind the ﬁrewall is
the internal network which is not accessible from the VPN connection.
On the external network, a honeypot acting as a router is also deployed.
For the six instances with ﬂags, 3 are before the ﬁrewall (Instance #1–3)
and the other 3 are behind it (Instance #4–6), as shown in Fig. 2.
#### 3.2. The Meta Attack Language (MAL)

study is based on, was designed to enable automatic generation of at-

to enable security assessments of system architectures. MAL can be
used to deﬁne domain-speciﬁc languages for diﬀerent domains, such
as industrial control systems (ICSs), internet of things (IoTs), vehicles,

etc. Conceptually, a MAL speciﬁcation of a system consists of diﬀerent
assets (e.g., Host), attack steps on the assets (e.g., Host.Compro-
mise), defenses on assets (e.g., Host.EndPointDetection) and ﬁnally
associations between diﬀerent assets. Additionally, to encode at-
tack graph logic, attack steps are connected to each other, which means
that the successful compromise of one attack step enables attempts at
other attack steps. Attack steps can be of two types, OR or AND, respec-
tively indicating that compromise of any individual parental attack step
is required (OR) or compromise of all parental attack steps is required
(AND) for the current step of the attack to be performed. Moreover, de-
fenses are related to attack steps, and if enabled, act as obstacles to the
connected attack steps. Additionally, a probability distribution, called
the local Time-To-Compromise (TTC), can optionally be associated to
each attack step, representing the diﬃculty of succeeding with that at-
tack step. The global TTC, on the other hand, represents the diﬃculty,
expressed in time, required for an attacker to succeed with a complete
attack. Defense steps are probability distributions over Boolean values
representing if they are active or not.
MAL-based languages are used to generate attack graphs that de-
scribe the possible attack paths to compromise the assets that make up
a model, instantiated in the language. Those attack paths are presented
as a graph where each node of the graph is an attack step required for
continued progress along the path.
In Fig. 3, the basic modeling MAL pipeline’s four steps are depicted.
It begins with a given MAL-based language from which the asset in-
stance model is derived. This leads to the generation of the complete
attack graph, representing all possible attack paths within the modeled
infrastructure. The graph can then be pruned to reveal the shortest path
to a speciﬁc attack goal.

#### 4.1. Ground truth measures of relative attack path diﬃculty
In this section, we will present the process by which we collected
and constructed the ground truth data of relative attack path diﬃculty
in our analysis.
The penetration testing training data from our cyber range is derived
from three distinct sources: i) Students A – 225 master’s level stu-
dents at KTH Royal Institute of Technology who undertook the course
in the autumn of 2021 and performed all hacks necessary to capture all
ﬂags and pass the course; ii) Students B – 38 students who enrolled
in a variant of the course during the same period (though the cyber
range and assignments remained identical) and provided feedback on
the perceived diﬃculty of each hack/ﬂag, by selecting among ﬁve dif-
ferent diﬃculty levels in an ordinal scale, as well as the duration taken
to capture each ﬂag; iii) Our own assessments of ﬂag diﬃculty, as we
are the cyber range maintainers and course instructors.
The collected data from Students A contain the grades that each
student got in the course for each ﬂag. For each ﬂag, a number of on-
demand hints are available, which oﬀer guidance to assist in ﬁnding
and capturing the respective ﬂag. The grade a student will get for each
captured ﬂag will be reduced in the case the student requests hints for
that speciﬁc ﬂag. By inverting the grade of each student for each ﬂag,
we were able to calculate the hint points required to capture that ﬂag.
Then, by calculating the average hint points needed for each of the 17
ﬂags, we established a measure of diﬃculty, represented by this average
hint point value. Based on our experience with the course, some of the
students request hints for ﬂags due to time constraints or indiﬀerence to
the grade rather than diﬃculty, we have opted to include data only from
the top 80% of Students A in our calculations in order to counteract
this.
To compute the diﬀerence in the number of required hint points
between any two targets A and B, we considered any intermediate steps
needed, utilizing our existing knowledge of the cyber range. This was
achieved by simply summing up the average hint points needed for each
target and calculating the diﬀerence. If for example target A is the ﬁrst
one and requires in average 1 hint point, target C is another target that
requires in average 4 hint points, and target B is the intermediate ﬂag
that requires in average 2 hint points and has to be captured before
reaching target C, then the hint points needed for target C would be
1+2+4=7 and the diﬀerence from target A to target C would be 1-7=-
6. We already know how the ﬂags are arranged and the intermediate
steps required for each from the cyber range.
To determine the relative ease or diﬃculty of achieving an attack
goal A compared to another attack goal B, we utilize a numerical value
from our own adapted version of Saaty’s 1–9 scale (Saaty, 1984), with
values in the range [0,8] where a value of 0 represents that both attack
goals are equally easy to achieve, and a value of 8 represents that attack
goal A is exceptionally easier than attack goal B. We call that Scale of
Ease.
By applying the process described above to all pairwise comparisons
and after normalizing the results to the [0,8] range, we can represent
the relative diﬃculties on our Scale of Ease.
From the second data source, namely the diﬃculty feedback pro-
vided for the ﬂags by Students B, we can adopt a similar approach.
However, this time we apply it to the diﬃculty scores instead of the
hint points needed for each ﬂag. This was facilitated by assigning a nu-
merical value to each of the diﬃculty scores available (very easy, easy,
moderately easy/diﬃcult, diﬃcult and impossible) and then calculating
the average of the student responses for each ﬂag. Consequently, we can
calculate the diﬀerential diﬃculty for all of our pairwise comparisons,
with the same approach as for the hint points using the necessary inter-
mediate steps to reach a ﬂag, and once again, normalize these values to
the [0,8] range.
Finally, for our own assessments for the relative diﬃculty of each
ﬂag, we can directly provide our estimated diﬃculty scores, and given
again the intermediate steps required to reach a ﬂag, calculate the an-
swer values for the pairwise questions.

#### 4.2. Experimental session setup
The experimental session has been designed to maximize the avail-
able comparison points by considering three key dimensions. These
dimensions are: i) the size of the network infrastructure that the as-
sessors interact with at each time, ii) the time that the assessors have
available to analyze the network infrastructure, and iii) the amoun-
t/level of information that was shared with the assessors before their
assessment.
The experiment is divided up into four sub-experiments, namely
Experiment 1–4, where the three dimensions (size of network infras-
tructure, available time, and level of information) are varied. This is
done because we aim to study how those three dimensions aﬀect the
accuracy of each assessor’s assessments.
In the experimental session, we requested each assessor to answer
numerous pairwise comparison questions. These queries were centered
on whether speciﬁc targets or attack objectives in the network infras-
tructure were easier to reach or achieve in comparison to others. They
should consider those questions for an attacker that is a professional
penetration tester and only has access to the cyber range via the VPN
connection.
Our aim was to obtain responses from all assessors to the posed ques-
tions, enabling us to compare their answers. Furthermore, as described
in Subsection 4.1, we now possess knowledge about the correct answers
to the questions. Consequently, we have prior knowledge regarding the
feasibility and relative diﬃculty of achieving speciﬁc attack goals com-
pared to others. Additionally, during the experimental design phase,
we had not yet created the coreLang model of the infrastructure which
means that the accuracy of simulation results for the speciﬁc attack
goals in question was at that point unknown to us.
To determine the relative ease or diﬃculty of achieving an attack
goal A compared to another attack goal B, the assessors utilize the pre-
viously presented Scale of Ease.
In the subsections following, how this is achieved for all types of
assessors will be described in detail.
To ensure a fair comparison, the same level and type of informa-
tion is consistently provided to both the human expert and the attack
simulation assessor. Additionally, the experiments were designed with
the requirement to be completed within a single day to accommodate
the participation of the human expert assessors. As already mentioned,
the three dimensions have been used in four diﬀerent experiments and
details about how each dimension was used in each one of the four
experiments is summarized in Table 2. Then, adjusting the designed ex-
periments to the human experts and to the attack simulation assessors
would be a straightforward task to perform and is described in Subsec-
tion 4.5 and Subsection 4.6, respectively.
#### 4.3. Method for collecting and analyzing the assessors’ data
In this study, data from diﬀerent types of assessors were collected
through their answers to pairwise comparison questions regarding the
ease of achieving speciﬁc attack goals for a professional penetration
tester. With a total of 17 attack goals available in the cyber range, the
number of unique pairwise comparison questions that could be asked
for k elements is given by the formula: k(k-1)/2. This results in a to-
tal of 136 pairwise comparisons. Asking all 136 questions to the human
experts would be impractical, so we selected a subset of 5 pairwise com-
parison questions for Experiment 1, 9 pairwise comparison questions
for Experiments 2 and 3, and 24 pairwise comparison questions for the
longest Experiment 4, considering the time available for the assessors to
avoid overwhelming them with too many questions. The question selec-
tion process followed a mixed approach, considering comparisons that
made sense for the experts (e.g., comparing attack goals with varying
diﬃculty) and ensuring that each of the included attack goals, in each
experiment, had at least one question.
All the collected data from the assessors are stored as answers to
the pairwise comparison questions. For example, a data point could
be represented as {ﬂag A, ﬂag B}=4, indicating that ﬂag A is easier
compared to ﬂag B with a score of 4 on our Scale of Ease. Another
example would be {ﬂag C, ﬂag D}=-7, implying that ﬂag D is easier
than ﬂag C with a value of 7.
In the analysis and comparison of the collected data, which are in
the format of the Scale of Ease and range from 0 to 8, we directly com-
pared the answer values. To assess correctness, we employed the root
mean square error (RMSE) as a measure. RMSE is widely used to eval-
uate the accuracy of estimators or assessors and is preferred over mean
square error (MSE) because it provides an error metric on the same

#### 4.4. Random guesser benchmark
To gain further insights and provide a meaningful comparison, we
also included a random guesser in our study, following the approach de-
scribed in Schmidhuber et al. (2001). The purpose of comparing against
random guessers was to evaluate whether the human experts and the
simulation results outperformed random chance or not. The random
guesser in our study was implemented as a pseudo-random number gen-
erator. It generated 50 diﬀerent random sets of answers to the same set
of questions that were given to the other assessors. For each random
guess iteration, the root mean square error (RMSE) was calculated by
comparing the random guesses against the ground truth. The average
RMSE of the random guesser was then computed and compared with
the RMSE values of the human experts and the simulation results.

##### 4.5.3. The human domain experts
In our experiments, a total of twelve human experts from the cyber-
security domain participated. Nine of these individuals originated from
the private sector, whereas the remaining three were from academia.
The expert practitioners occupied various roles, extending from sea-

We conducted twelve individual experiment sessions with the ex-

background checks, performed by us, designed to verify their compe-

### 7. Discussion
Threat modeling is a promising approach for assessing the cyberse-
curity of large ICT infrastructures, and one way to achieve this is by
using domain-speciﬁc modeling languages to create models and per-
form cybersecurity assessments. In this study, we proposed and applied
a method to corroborate coreLang, a speciﬁc domain-speciﬁc language
for threat modeling in the ICT domain, through an attempt at falsiﬁca-
tion.
Firstly, we assume that had the domain-speciﬁc language for this
study been tailored speciﬁcally to the domain, since coreLang was cre-
ated to be a ﬁt for modeling any ICT infrastructure and therefore has
a high level of modeling abstraction, the simulation results would have
been more precise and detailed. Adapting the languages to align with
the domain’s unique needs could enhance performance and more aptly
assess the security of cyber infrastructures.
Furthermore, a potential concern for this study might be the lim-
ited time allotted to the human experts. It is conceivable that given
more time, these experts could provide markedly improved assessments
of the infrastructure’s cybersecurity. However, the time constraint was
set based on our experts’ maximum availability, and experiments were
accordingly designed to ﬁt within that timeframe. Moreover, after each
experimental session, we inquired about our experts’ conﬁdence in their
responses. On average, they expressed moderate to high conﬁdence in
their answers for all experiments, with the exception of Experiment 1,
which was tightly time constrained by design.
Furthermore, a plausible objection to this study might arise due to
our role as the developers of MAL and coreLang. It could be raised as a
concern that our involvement could potentially introduce bias into the
study’s design or the modeling process. For instance, skepticism might
arise about the experiment’s fairness or whether the infrastructure’s
model created by coreLang was deliberately tailored to yield favorable
simulation results. It is important to remind that in order to mitigate
potential bias, during the experimental design phase, we had not yet
created the coreLang models of the infrastructure. Having a coreLang
developer involved in the modeling process ensured the language was
used as intended. Ideally, the language would be entirely intuitive, pre-
venting modelers from inadvertently creating models that do not mirror
reality. While this usability aspect is not addressed in our study, the
coreLang developer’s participation helped mitigate the occasional lack
of intuitiveness when modeling with coreLang.
In addition, it is important to address aspects of validity, reliability,
and generality in the context of this study. One potential factor aﬀect-
ing reliability is the selection of human domain experts, as the study
outcomes are closely tied to their participation. To mitigate this poten-
tial concern, we have involved a substantial number of experts in our
study. However, it is worth noting that if more time had been avail-
able, then we could have included an even larger group of experts, that
would further enhance the reliability of our results.
Another crucial aspect of reliability to consider is our reliance on a
single, experimental cyber range for our assessed infrastructure, which
contained a limited number of vulnerabilities. It’s possible that utilizing
a diﬀerent cyber range might have yielded varied results. Nonetheless,
we believe that the experimental cyber range employed in this study is
quite realistic and includes a fairly representative set of vulnerabilities
that our assessors were tasked to security assess.
In terms of the simulation results, both validity and reliability are
ensured, given that the simulation’s assessments would remain consis-
tent for the same modeled infrastructure and the same attack goals.
Regarding the validity of our student data, it is important to clar-
ify that while the human domain experts are considered cybersecurity
professionals, and have the expertise to understand how a penetration
tester would act, the students who represent the ground truth are not
professional penetration testers. They did however have in their dis-
posal a lot of guidance by the on-demand hints and other resources
provided to them during the course, which made them able to success-
fully capture all the ﬂags. The data collected from them are used to

Our interest in the relative diﬃculty of attack paths makes that data
more relevant to us. For that reason, it is not necessary that the stu-
dents are equally proﬁcient as professional hackers, but only that both
groups have similar perceptions of relative diﬃculty. So, for example,
even though professional attackers might complete the binary exploita-
tion in shorter time than students, both would likely ﬁnd that more
diﬃcult than brute forcing an FTP server with a very simple numeric
password.
Another potential validity concern is the way we asked questions
to our assessors. While we chose pairwise comparison questions, an al-
ternative approach could involve asking assessors to provide absolute
time estimates for each attack goal. However, we opted for pairwise
comparisons because coreLang, as an abstract modeling language, does
not provide high-resolution time estimates for individual attack steps,
and because evaluating the relative diﬃculty of various attacks holds
greater signiﬁcance for practical decision-making, as it necessitates pri-
oritizing security concerns that require attention.
Touching on our study’s generality, we posit that altering the infras-
tructure to be assessed by the human experts and by coreLang would
not substantially impact the results because coreLang can adequately
model and simulate attacks on any ICT infrastructure.
Additionally, the selection of our human experts is a topic for dis-
cussion and a weakness of our study. As mentioned earlier, the experts
who responded to our time-bounded open call for participation were
qualitatively assessed by us as researchers, without using a formal as-
sessment method. This approach was driven by two primary reasons.
First, we aimed to compare the language against an average cybersecu-
rity professional, positing this as a benchmark to showcase where the
language begins to oﬀer beneﬁts. Second, establishing a reference group
of higher expertise would have presented logistical challenges, both in
appropriately qualifying individuals and in recruiting participants will-
ing to dedicate time and eﬀort to the experiment. Another point we
can make is that the expertise required from our participants was not
proﬁciency in penetration testing, but rather the ability to conduct a
high-level security analysis. For example, an expert’s lack of skill in ex-
ecuting an SQL injection attack does not deem them less suitable for
our study.
Finally, revisiting our hypothesis that analyzing an infrastructure of
increased complexity would signiﬁcantly impede the human experts’ as-
sessment process, we can conclude that this is still an open question to
us since our results were inconclusive towards that direction. To un-
derstand exactly how much and under what circumstances the human
experts perform signiﬁcantly worse than simulations, would require an-
other experimental setup.
### 8. Conclusion
The most important conclusion drawn from this study is that the
simulation results generated using coreLang outperform on average the
assessments performed by human domain experts.
Another noteworthy conclusion is that a small number of human
experts can still handle the challenging task of assessing a large and ini-
tially unknown infrastructure within a limited timeframe and individ-
ually achieve a higher level of accuracy in their assessments compared
to the rest of the group. When given more time, some of the human
experts are able to provide better assessments that are comparable or
slightly better than the simulation’s assessments.
Another conclusion is that the eﬃciency of the simulation’s assess-
ments, that are being generated almost instantly, cannot be matched by
the human domain experts. Furthermore, both human domain experts
as well as simulations are signiﬁcantly better than random guessers in
their assessments.
As a closing remark, it is worth mentioning that while coreLang
has been validated and shown to be, on average, better than human
domain experts, there is still room for further improvements to make its
assessments even more realistic. Nevertheless, this study demonstrates
that progress is being made in the right direction.
